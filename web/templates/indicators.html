{# ===== MOVING AVERAGES ===== #}

Moving Averages (SMA)

What it is

A Simple Moving Average (SMA) is the average closing price over a set number of days. The two most common are the 50-day SMA (short-term trend) and the 200-day SMA (long-term trend).

Think of it as a "smoothed" version of the price — it filters out daily noise and shows you the underlying direction.

How to read it

Bullish Price above 50-day SMA — short-term uptrend
Bullish Price above 200-day SMA — long-term uptrend
Bearish Price below 50-day SMA — short-term downtrend
Bearish Price below 200-day SMA — long-term downtrend

Dashboard signals generated

Price above 50MA Price below 50MA Golden Cross Death Cross Above 200DMA Below 200DMA

Golden Cross & Death Cross

These are crossover signals — when one moving average crosses another:

  • Golden Cross: The 50-day SMA crosses above the 200-day SMA. This is a classic bullish signal suggesting the trend has shifted from bearish to bullish. Historically, stocks tend to rally after a golden cross — but it's a lagging indicator (the move has often already started).
  • Death Cross: The 50-day SMA crosses below the 200-day SMA. The opposite — suggests a shift to a bearish trend. Can signal the start of a prolonged decline, but also produces false signals in sideways markets.

The 200-day SMA in particular

The 200-day SMA is the single most widely watched moving average by institutional investors. Many fund managers use it as a "line in the sand":

  • Stocks trading above their 200-day SMA are considered to be in an uptrend — institutions are more willing to buy
  • Stocks trading below are in a downtrend — institutions become cautious, selling pressure increases
  • The 200-day SMA often acts as support (price bounces off it in an uptrend) or resistance (price fails to break above it in a downtrend)

On the signal cards, the Momentum section shows whether each stock is above or below its 200-day SMA and by how much.

Limitations

  • Moving averages are lagging — they tell you where the trend has been, not where it's going
  • In sideways/choppy markets, the price whipsaws back and forth across the SMA, generating false signals
  • They work best in trending markets — strong uptrends or downtrends
  • A golden cross doesn't mean "buy now" — the best entry might have been weeks earlier when the price bottomed
{# ===== RSI ===== #}

RSI (Relative Strength Index)

What it is

RSI measures momentum — how fast and how much the price has been rising or falling over the last 14 days. It produces a number from 0 to 100.

It answers: "Has this stock been going up too fast (overbought) or down too fast (oversold)?"

How to read it

RSI < 30 Oversold — price has fallen hard, may be due for a bounce
RSI 30-70 Normal range — no extreme signal
RSI > 70 Overbought — price has risen fast, may be due for a pullback

Dashboard signals generated

RSI Oversold RSI Overbought

Important nuances

  • RSI oversold ≠ "buy now": A stock can stay oversold for weeks in a strong downtrend. Oversold means the selling has been aggressive, not that it's finished. It's a watch signal, not an action signal.
  • Strong stocks stay overbought: In a bull market, quality stocks can have RSI above 70 for months. Selling just because RSI is high can mean selling your winners too early.
  • Ex-dividend distortion: When a stock goes ex-dividend, the price drops by the dividend amount. This can push RSI down artificially. The dashboard suppresses RSI signals within 3 days of an ex-dividend date to avoid false alerts.
  • Best used with context: RSI oversold on a high-quality stock in an uptrend = potential buying opportunity. RSI oversold on a deteriorating business = could keep falling.

On the watchlist, the RSI column is colour-coded: green for oversold (≤ 30), red for overbought (≥ 70).

{# ===== MACD ===== #}

MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence)

What it is

MACD measures the relationship between two moving averages (12-day and 26-day EMA). It shows whether the short-term trend is strengthening or weakening relative to the longer-term trend.

The MACD line is compared to a "signal line" (9-day EMA of the MACD). When they cross, it generates a signal.

How to read it

Crossover MACD crosses above signal line — momentum shifting bullish
Crossunder MACD crosses below signal line — momentum shifting bearish

Dashboard signals generated

MACD Crossover MACD Crossunder

How to think about MACD

  • MACD is a trend-following momentum indicator — it works best in trending markets, not sideways ones
  • A bullish crossover after a prolonged decline is more significant than one during choppy sideways action
  • Divergence is a powerful (advanced) signal: if the stock makes a new low but MACD makes a higher low, it suggests selling pressure is weakening — potential reversal coming
  • MACD is lagging like all moving average-based indicators — it confirms trends rather than predicting them

Settings used: 12-day fast EMA, 26-day slow EMA, 9-day signal line (standard defaults).

{# ===== BOLLINGER BANDS ===== #}

Bollinger Bands

What it is

Bollinger Bands are a volatility envelope around the 20-day SMA. The upper band is 2 standard deviations above, the lower band is 2 below. As volatility increases, the bands widen; as it decreases, they contract.

About 95% of price action falls within the bands under normal conditions.

How to read it

Touch lower band Price at lower extreme — potentially oversold (in an uptrend)
Touch upper band Price at upper extreme — potentially overbought
Squeeze Bands very narrow — low volatility, often precedes a big move (direction unknown)

Key concept: Bollinger Band Squeeze

When the bands contract tightly, it means the stock has been trading in a very narrow range. This low-volatility period often precedes a breakout — the stock will eventually make a big move. The bands don't tell you which direction, just that a move is coming. Combine with other indicators (RSI, MACD, fundamentals) to gauge direction.

Settings used: 20-day SMA, 2 standard deviations.

{# ===== VOLUME ===== #}

Volume

What it is

Volume is the number of shares traded in a day. The dashboard tracks the volume ratio — today's volume compared to the 20-day average.

Volume confirms price moves. A big price move on high volume is more meaningful than one on low volume.

How to read it

Spike > 2x Volume spike — something is happening, investigate (earnings, news, institutional buying/selling)

Dashboard signals generated

Volume Spike

Why volume matters

  • Price up + high volume = strong conviction buying — bullish
  • Price down + high volume = strong conviction selling — bearish
  • Price up + low volume = weak rally, potentially unsustainable
  • Price down + low volume = lack of sellers, may not be meaningful

Volume spikes often occur around earnings announcements, index rebalances, or significant news. The dashboard flags spikes above 2x the 20-day average as an "attention" signal so you can investigate.

{# ===== 52-WEEK RANGE ===== #}

52-Week Range

What it is

The 52-week high and low represent the highest and lowest prices the stock has traded at in the past year. The dashboard shows where the current price sits as a percentage of this range.

How to read it

Within 5% of low Near 52-week low — potential entry point if fundamentals are sound
Within 3% of high Near 52-week high — may be extended, consider if there's still upside

Dashboard signals generated

Near 52-week Low Near 52-week High

Context matters

  • Near 52-week low on a quality company with solid fundamentals = potential opportunity. The market may be overreacting to short-term news.
  • Near 52-week low on a deteriorating business = could be a value trap. "Cheap" doesn't mean "good value" if earnings are declining.
  • Near 52-week high doesn't automatically mean "sell" — many great stocks spend most of their time near all-time highs because they keep growing.

The range bar on the watchlist gives you a quick visual of where the stock sits within its 52-week range.

{# ===== FUNDAMENTAL SIGNALS ===== #}

Fundamental Signals

The Signal Cards page evaluates each watchlist stock across four fundamental categories. Here's what each metric means and why it matters.

A) Quality

ROE (Return on Equity)

How much profit a company generates for each dollar of shareholder equity. Higher = better.

≥ 15%High ROE — excellent capital allocation
8-15%Moderate — acceptable for most sectors
< 8%Low — the business isn't generating strong returns
Profit Margins

Net income as a percentage of revenue. Wide margins mean pricing power and efficiency.

≥ 20%Strong — indicates competitive moat or premium product
10-20%Healthy — adequate for most industries
< 10%Thin — vulnerable to cost pressures
Debt to Equity

Total debt divided by shareholder equity. Measures financial leverage and balance sheet risk.

< 50%Strong balance sheet — conservative financing
50-100%Moderate debt — manageable for most businesses
> 150%High leverage — risk flag, especially in rising rate environments

B) Growth

Revenue Growth

Year-over-year change in total revenue. Shows whether the business is expanding.

Earnings Growth (EPS)

Year-over-year change in earnings per share. More important than revenue growth because it shows profitability is growing, not just sales.

Earnings Revisions

Whether analyst estimates are being revised up (upgrades) or down (downgrades). Detected by comparing forward PE to trailing PE. This is one of the most powerful signals — stocks with earnings upgrades tend to outperform over 3-12 months.

C) Valuation

PE Ratio (Price to Earnings)

Share price divided by earnings per share. How much you're paying for each dollar of earnings. Lower ≠ always better — fast-growing companies deserve higher PEs.

< 12xLow PE — may be undervalued (or earnings are about to decline)
12-25xFair range for most ASX companies
> 35xExpensive — needs strong growth to justify
Free Cash Flow (FCF) Yield

Free cash flow divided by market cap. Tells you how much actual cash the business generates relative to its price. This is arguably more important than PE because earnings can be manipulated but cash is real.

Dividend Yield

Annual dividends per share divided by the share price. For medium-to-long term investors, dividends compound significantly. ASX stocks with franking credits are even more tax-effective.

D) Momentum

Relative Strength vs ASX200

3-month return of the stock minus the 3-month return of the ASX200. Positive = outperforming the index. Stocks with strong relative strength tend to continue outperforming (momentum factor).

3M and 6M Returns

Recent price performance. Combined with fundamental signals, helps you judge whether the market agrees with the fundamentals (or disagrees — which could be an opportunity).

{# ===== HOW SIGNAL CARDS WORK ===== #}

How Signal Cards Work

The Signals page generates a card for each watchlist stock by evaluating all the metrics above and scoring them.

Scoring

Each signal tag is classified as bullish (+1), neutral (0), or bearish (-1). Risk flags subtract an additional -0.5 each. The composite score determines the overall rating:

Score ≥ 3Bullish rating
Score -1 to +2Neutral rating
Score ≤ -2Bearish rating

Action Labels

BUY CANDIDATEBullish rating + 0-1 risk flags + not extremely overvalued
WATCHLISTNeutral rating, or bullish but too expensive to buy now
AVOIDBearish rating or 3+ risk flags

Important caveats

  • Signal cards are a screening tool, not a buy/sell recommendation. Always do your own research.
  • The data comes from yfinance and may have delays or inaccuracies — always verify key numbers.
  • Fundamental data is backward-looking — it tells you about the last reporting period, not the next one.
  • Sector context matters — a 10% margin is excellent for a supermarket but poor for a software company.
  • No single metric tells the full story. The power is in the combination of quality + growth + valuation + momentum.
